The raw material

What is a signal?

A signal is any quantity that changes over time (or space) and carries information. A microphone turns tiny air-pressure changes into a voltage; an ECG records the electrical activity of the heart; a thermometer logs temperature. Before any processing, that sequence of measurements is the raw signal.

〰Continuous-time

In nature a signal has a value at every instant — a smooth function of time.

⋮Discrete-time

A computer stores a list of numbers, one every Ts seconds: the samples.

⚡Amplitude & energy

The vertical axis is amplitude. Its average power is measured by the RMS value.

Counting cycles

What is a hertz (Hz)?

Frequency counts how many complete cycles a repeating signal makes in one second. 1 Hz = one cycle per second. A sine wave is the shadow of a point rotating on a circle: one full turn is one cycle. Your ear hears roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz.

fFrequency

Cycles per second, measured in hertz (Hz).

TPeriod

The duration of one cycle. Higher frequency → shorter period.

λWavelength in air

Sound travels at about 343 m/s, so a 343 Hz tone has a 1 m wavelength.

From clicks to pitch

Play a train of clicks and slowly raise the rate. Below about 15 Hz you hear separate clicks; above about 30 Hz your brain fuses them into a musical tone. Pitch is frequency.

“Hertz” simply means “per second”. A heart beating 72 times per minute beats at 72 ÷ 60 = 1.2 Hz; the A key on a piano vibrates 440 times per second = 440 Hz.

03
Analog → digital

Sampling, Nyquist & bits

A computer cannot store a continuous curve. It samples it fs times per second and quantizes each sample to one of 2b levels. Sample too slowly and a fast wave masquerades as a slower one — this is aliasing. The Nyquist–Shannon theorem says fs must be more than twice the highest frequency. That is why CDs use 44,100 Hz and speech systems often use 16,000 Hz.

Time domain ↔ frequency domain

The Fourier transform

The Fourier transform answers one question for every frequency f: how much of f is inside my signal? Multiply the signal by a rotating arrow e−j2πft — that is, wrap it around a circle at speed f. If f matches a hidden component the wrapped curve becomes lopsided and its centre of mass jumps away from the origin. Sweep f and you get the spectrum.

06
Frequency over time

The spectrogram (STFT)

Real sounds change over time — one spectrum of the whole clip blurs everything together. The Short-Time Fourier Transform cuts the signal into short overlapping frames, multiplies each by a window, and takes an FFT per frame. Placing the spectra side by side gives a spectrogram: time runs left to right, frequency bottom to top, and colour shows energy.

07
Hearing like a human

Mel spectrogram, MFCC & other features

Our ears do not perceive frequency linearly: the jump from 100 Hz to 200 Hz sounds huge, while 10,000 Hz to 10,100 Hz is barely noticeable. The Mel scale warps frequency to match perceived pitch. A bank of triangular filters, narrow at low frequencies and wide at high ones, pools the spectrogram into a few dozen Mel bands. Take the log and a DCT and you obtain MFCCs — the classic compact features behind speech recognition and speaker identification.
